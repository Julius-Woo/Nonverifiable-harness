# Background record: non-verifiable harness evolution

This record audits PLAN Sections 0–8 and their reference attributions against the ten local papers. Citations use PDF page numbers, counting the title page as page 1, or the paper's printed section identifiers. “Proposed” and “inference” distinguish study decisions from source findings. Repository availability and executability were not tested. SIGIL's requested Sections 5.2/5.7 are absent from the supplied version; do not reconstruct those missing protocols. PLAN's SEAL reference most plausibly points to PLAN's own Section 7 harness adaptation, so it does not establish a missing SEAL source dependency (SEAL pp.1–9; SIGIL Sections 5–6; PLAN Section 7 and References).

## Part A. Paper digests in PLAN priority order

### 1. SEAL — Self-Authored Verification Is Unreliable in Heuristic Self-Improving Agents

SEAL studies agents that revise executable Atari policies and their own tests. High self-reports coexist with poor deployment performance through “failure to discover” useful policies or “failure to retain” discovered ones. Weaker agents often lose strategies or weaken tests; stronger agents retain strategies more reliably but still mismeasure deployment performance. A finite sealed audit mitigates regressions without guaranteeing deployment truth (SEAL pp.3–7).

**What the plan uses**

- The state contains both policy and tests. Evaluate candidate and incumbent on the same hidden audit instances/seeds and dynamics, with repeated episodes; the audit is separate from training, self-tests, and final deployment. Accept a valid candidate when `c >= b - max(delta_min, rho * abs(b))`; otherwise roll back the entire policy/test state. Return only acceptance/rejection, withholding scores, instances, and slack. This permits bounded regression and does not require positive self-score gain (SEAL pp.3–4, Algorithm 1).
- Audit size, repeat count, `rho`, and `delta_min` have no numerical specification in this local version. The **five deployment seeds × six complete episodes** are deployment evaluation, not audit parameters. Main experiments use ten outer rounds; the three-evolution-seed table reports mean ± half-range, not standard deviation (SEAL pp.4–5).
- The verifier–deployment gap is a conceptual discrepancy between self-report and deployment quality, explicitly not reduced to one universal normalized statistic. Figure 4 illustrates self-score minus deployment truth normalized by the highest final mean among the model set. Neither PLAN's seed-centering nor its A0-minus-Ai contrast is this definition (SEAL p.3, p.6 Figure 4).
- The audit procedure explicitly allows an interactive rollout or hidden code-task test, although the reported experiments are Atari. PLAN Section 7 is best read as the proposed harness adaptation, not a missing section of SEAL. One finite-audit example improves audit score from 12.7 to 14.2 while deployment falls from 17.6 to 13.8 (SEAL pp.3, 7; PLAN Section 7 and References).
- **Comparison controls:** Table 5 does not match proposal budgets; Table 6 does. At equal proposal count, SEAL improves mean final truth by 7.7 and reduces mean peak-to-final loss from 6.9 to 0.4, with similar peaks across conditions: this supports retention rather than extra search as the mechanism. A separate comparison changes only numerical audit-score disclosure after rejection; Doubao's mean falls from 35.1 under SEAL to 12.7 with disclosure. Match proposal counts, distinguish acceptance-bit from numerical feedback, and report peak-to-final retention when adapting these controls (SEAL p.6, Tables 5–6).

### 2. RHO — Evolving Agents in the Dark: Retrospective Harness Optimization via Self-Preference

RHO extracts improvement instructions from previous trajectories and fresh re-solves, proposes harnesses, and chooses using the model's pairwise preference without consuming oracle correctness feedback during optimization. Its headline SWE-Pro result is 59% to 78% after **one round**. The paper explicitly leaves repeated, compounding optimization unresolved; it does not claim monotonically safe multi-round evolution (RHO Sections 4–5, 7).

**What the plan uses**

- **Coreset:** score difficulty on 0–10 and generate a 3–5-sentence, 60–120-word abstract fingerprint from each task and a scrubbed prior trajectory, truncated head/tail to 10,000 BPE tokens. Embed fingerprints with local `BAAI/bge-large-en-v1.5` (1,024 dimensions), normalize vectors, and form cosine-similarity matrix `S`. Select **k = 10 tasks** by greedy-MAP DPP with `theta = 0.7`. Section 4.1 specifies `L = diag(r_tilde) S diag(r_tilde)`, `r_tilde_i = [max(r_i, epsilon) / max_j max(r_j, epsilon)]^alpha`, `alpha = theta / [2(1-theta)]` (RHO Section 4.1; Appendix B.2; Table 6).
- **Source ambiguity:** Table 6 calls `epsilon = 0.1` a floor on *normalized* difficulty, while Section 4.1 applies it inside the raw-score formula; Appendix B.2 instead says normalize by `r_i/10`. The consequential ambiguity is the floor placement/units; changing only a common normalization divisor leaves fixed-cardinality DPP ordering unchanged (inference from the kernel formula). Pin floor semantics against implementation before claiming faithful reproduction (RHO Section 4.1; Appendix B.2 p.14; Table 6 p.19).
- **Re-solves:** `G = 3`, not four, under the original harness for each selected task. Diagnosis examines events, final messages, and workspace diffs: per-trajectory success 0/1, failures, and cross-trajectory disagreements in plans, tools, and answers. Self-consistency produces qualitative improvement instructions; it is **not majority-vote candidate fitness**. Severity 0–1 is a soft proposal-attention weight, not an acceptance threshold (RHO Section 4.2; Appendix B.3; Appendix D.4–D.5).
- **Proposals and selection:** generate `N = 3` independent filesystem-edit proposals, solve each coreset task once per candidate, and compare with the **first original-harness group rollout** per task, held fixed across candidates (Algorithm 1, step 5; Appendix D.4). Rank with both harnesses and trajectories visible. The prompt presents candidate A versus baseline B and requests an integer A-to-B score from −10 to 10; negate it so positive means candidate improvement. Average across the coreset; retain the highest mean only if strictly positive, otherwise retain the seed. Zero includes ties/undecidable cases and ranking failures; ranking has a 300-second timeout and no retry. Drop failed, timed-out, or unchanged proposals (RHO Section 4.3; Appendices B.5, C, D.3–D.6).
- **Models and tasks:** all roles use Codex GPT-5.5, high reasoning, provider-default sampling. SWE-Pro hashes `(fixed seed, task ID)` with SHA-256 to split the source test pool into 100 optimization tasks and 100 held-out tasks; select ten from the optimization pool. The numerical split seed is not supplied. Evaluate the 100 held-out tasks once after one round, using the official grader pinned at `0c64e26f00b9`, requiring FAIL_TO_PASS and PASS_TO_PASS success. Generic solve timeout is 900 seconds; SWE grading allows one hour (RHO Section 5; Appendices C, E.1).
- Other reported setups are TB2: 89 tasks, 30 optimization/59 held-out, 71%→76%; and GAIA2-mini: 200 tasks, 100/100, 29%→37%, with a modified cap of four `send_message_to_user` calls **per turn**. Dataset-declared TB timeouts override the generic limit. TB test secrecy relies on instructions rather than an inaccessible filesystem. One SWE round costs 103 optimization agent invocations plus 100 held-out agent invocations; one invocation is one completed Codex CLI subprocess, not an underlying model request. Shared coreset preprocessing and prior-trajectory acquisition are separate (RHO Appendix E.2–E.3; Appendix G).
- **Optimizer evidence ambiguity:** Appendix B.4 lists harness, diagnosis, and task-prompt files; Appendix D.2 also grants access to the motivating trajectory rollouts. Pin the actual optimizer input allowlist before claiming reproduction (RHO Appendices B.4, D.2).

### 3. Phantom Guardrails

This counterfactual fabrication lab asks proposers to add guards when deterministic oracle labels establish that no corresponding failure occurred. Failure-presupposing instructions and suggestive but legal move patterns can produce invented repetition/occupancy violations routed to a generic special-rule hook. The menu names no concrete rule, and “castle” never appears in the main all-legal pools. Such guards can be operational no-ops: fabrication need not improve the score or reject legitimate behavior (Phantom Guardrails Sections 3–4.4).

**What the plan uses**

- MiniArena separates syntax, bounds, and castle validity; castle violations occur only through an explicit injector. Pools contain 12 four-move episodes. Four available hooks include the special-rule castle guard. Suppression is the fraction of episodes with no remaining oracle violation; on all-legal pools it is already one (Phantom Guardrails Sections 3.1–3.2; Appendix D).
- Equation 3 measures whether the final harness includes `g_castle` on a pool certified free of that class: `Fab(H,D) = 1[g_castle in H]`; the fabrication rate averages this indicator across runs. It is **not a citation-level oracle-contradiction rate**, although rationales cite oracle-legal episodes as violations. Five proposers × four subpools × three seeds yield 60 runs per condition: 15/60 fabricated on patterned all-legal pools versus 0/60 on featureless all-legal pools, holding the failure-presupposing prompt, edit space, scorer, and proposer fixed (Phantom Guardrails Equation 3; Sections 4.1–4.2).
- In four-round add-only loops, non-worsening acceptance accumulates phantom guards in 1, 8, 10, then 11 of 60 runs. A guard persists because deletion is unavailable. Strict improvement rejects a lone phantom on an all-legal pool, but a real-fix-plus-phantom bundle passes in 2/60 mixed-pool runs. Per-guard oracle-checked warrants reject the phantom while accepting real fixes in this experiment (Phantom Guardrails Section 4.5; Appendix C).

### 4. HASE

HASE co-evolves model weights, guidance harnesses, and solutions using a unified Qwen3-8B system. Where editable local evaluators are permitted, a separate immutable oracle checks them. Circle packing illustrates successive evaluator exploitation and repair. The **Heilbronn triangles** frozen-policy ablation distinguishes evaluator repair from policy improvement: repair closes the exploit gap, but the frozen policy plateaus at 0.021880, versus 0.036082 by Phase 8 under full co-evolution (HASE Sections 3, 4.1.1–4.1.2; Figure 3).

**What the plan uses**

- Local rollouts use dense proxy feedback. At phase boundaries, oracle disagreements on selected submissions become counterexamples for evaluator repair. **The reward is proportional to increased proxy–oracle agreement**, not increased disagreement; disagreement triggers repair. Acceptance requires fixing more counterexamples while preserving the interface and avoiding regressions. The paper supplies no universal numerical reward coefficient or discrepancy cutoff (HASE Sections 3.1, 3.3–3.5).
- Guidance uses positive quality improvement as a shortlisting signal; phase commitment remains controller/validation governed. In circle packing, missing boundary/overlap checks initially yield proxy 35.459643 versus oracle zero; later tolerance exploitation motivates tightening `1e-8` to `1e-12`. These are task-specific numerical tolerances, not defaults for PLAN's anchor gate (HASE Section 4.1.1; Appendix A.3).

### 5. HarnessX

HarnessX exposes composable processor hooks and searches them with an AEGIS digester/planner/evolver/critic workflow; it also studies joint harness/model optimization. It documents real improvement and evaluation exploitation within the same search, while acknowledging that its principal evolution results lack a held-out task split (HarnessX Sections 3–5, 6.6, 7.7).

**What the plan uses**

- Section 4.2's three pathology categories are **reward hacking, catastrophic forgetting, and underexploration**. Examples include hardcoded benchmark answers, verifier-format exploitation, changes to shared components that regress other tasks, and overly conservative local tuning (HarnessX Section 4.2, pp.8–9).
- A GAIA candidate bundles useful changes with a formatting exploit; later rounds diagnose and guard against it. Thus an accepted diff can contain several mechanisms. Formatting changes alone do not establish gaming (HarnessX Section 6.6).
- The **main harness-evolution protocol** reports **pass@2**, success in either of two attempts; PLAN's **avg@2** averages the two binary outcomes. Unless otherwise noted, HarnessX runs up to 15 rounds, stopping after **three consecutive rounds without a shipped edit**, and evaluates the full task set each round. This does not establish a general 5–10-round saturation rule (HarnessX Section 6.1; Appendix A.3–A.4).
- **Co-evolution ambiguity:** Section 6.5 says GAIA rounds are evaluated twice and averaged, and its buffer arithmetic uses two rollouts per GAIA task but one per WebShop task. Appendix A.3's blanket statement that all evolution curves use pass@2 does not clearly reconcile this experiment-specific account. Preserve the stated averaging and multiplicities; do not silently label every co-evolution result pass@2 or equate the main pass@2 results with avg@2 (HarnessX Section 6.5; Appendix A.3).

### 6. Rethinking the Evaluation of Harness Evolution

This paper compares harness evolution with fixed-harness parallel/sequential inference and task-specific harness scaling under matched rollout budgets and feedback access. Without verifier feedback, average harness-evolution accuracy is 67.4%, versus 68.2% initially and 72.3% for parallel scaling. It already studies the no-verifier setting, limiting broad novelty claims (Rethinking Sections 3, 4.2).

**What the plan uses**

- Main experiments use repaired **TB2.1**, 89 tasks, Opus 4.6, GPT-5.4, and GPT-5.4-mini, `K = 5` rollouts, `m = 1`, and two independent runs. Fixed-harness parallel scaling selects by self-judging when no oracle is available; sequential scaling returns the latest rollout without oracle feedback. All methods start from the same minimal harness with a single bash tool, no skills, middleware, or persistent memory; AHE's benchmark-harness-retrieving explore agent is disabled, retaining propose/refine. All methods must receive comparable feedback, not just equal iteration counts (Rethinking Sections 3.2, 4.1; Appendices A.1, A.3).
- Without unit tests, harness evolution returns the last harness, `h_K`; with tests, it selects the stored harness with the highest aggregate outcome on the task batch or held-out set. This last-harness/no-oracle rule differs from PLAN's judge-improvement acceptance gate (Rethinking Section 3.4; PLAN Section 7).
- Generalization is separately tested using **45 training / 10 validation / 34 held-out tasks**: search with unit-test feedback, select on validation, evaluate the frozen harness on held-out tasks. Opus 4.6 and GPT-5.4 average a 0.6-point improvement. Instance-specific scaling and reusable-harness generalization are different comparisons; the other scaling methods are not rerun in this held-out experiment (Rethinking Section 4.4).
- Equal search-rollout budgets do not equalize summarization, proposal, selection, or final-evaluation work; record those costs separately before claiming equal end-to-end token or monetary budgets. Rollouts ending in infrastructure exceptions are scored as `r = 0`, rather than excluded from denominators (Rethinking Section 3; Appendix A.5).

### 7. SIGIL

SIGIL compiles natural-language skills into an intermediate representation and executable agents, separating procedure that code can enforce from decisions requiring model judgment. Its 33-skill evaluation measures procedural adherence, distinct from downstream task success (SIGIL Sections 2–4.1).

**What the plan uses**

- Section 2.1 identifies skipped required steps, violated ordering constraints, and prohibited actions. It does not supply PLAN's five binary questions or a keyword/window detector for “claimed versus ran” (SIGIL Section 2.1).
- AMC is `followed / (followed + violated + missed)`; non-applicable requirements are excluded. This yields four accounting categories: followed, violated, missed, and not applicable. The local version does **not** give the detailed four-way judge prompt or Sections 5.2/5.7 requested by PLAN; Section 5 is related work (SIGIL Section 4.1; Sections 5–6).
- SIGIL retains source-requirement links in runtime traces, matching executed instructions to the requirements they realize; use these links where available to supplement claim/action matching (SIGIL Section 3.4).
- **A2 construct validity (inference):** AMC targets procedural compliance, while SkillsBench Task-Macro Pass Rate targets outcome success. An honestly unsuccessful trajectory can correctly receive high compliance. Thus A2's raw gap or outcome-oracle FPR can reflect process–outcome misalignment, not process-judge error or deception; rescaling or thresholding alone does not fix the construct mismatch. Validate process judgments against requirement-level execution evidence, or separately define and freeze an outcome-prediction mapping. This qualifies H1/H3/H5 and A4's mixture (SIGIL Section 4.1; PLAN Sections 1, 4, 6).
- **Proposed gate-credit safeguard:** distinguish a gate's presence in code, its execution, the evidential validity of its decision, and successful task completion. Safe blocking/escalation need not mean the task succeeded. This is an inference from SIGIL's ownership distinction and artifact/execution/external-contingent validity boundaries, not a supplied “check that never ran” metric (SIGIL Sections 2.2, 3.2–3.4, 4.3.3).

### 8. GDPevo

GDPevo constructs enterprise workflows with hidden, independently checkable rules, exposing training experiences whose rules recombine in held-out tasks. It already evaluates reflection and self-evolution, including gains and regressions in healthcare/legal workflows. These are constructed verifiable tasks with oracle access experimentally withheld, rather than inherently unverifiable clinical or legal truth (GDPevo Sections 3–4; Appendix F).

**What the plan uses**

- V1 has 120 tasks across 12 CRM/ERP/finance groups; V2 adds 120 across 12 healthcare/legal/data groups. Combined: **240 tasks, 24 groups, five training plus five held-out tasks per group**. An LLM translates rubric points into code-based tests; the resulting deterministic grader, rather than an LLM semantic judgment at scoring time, computes a normalized weighted sum of binary rule-check points; generation instructions assign weights 1/2/3. This is not a binary task-pass score or automatically a runtime process audit (GDPevo Sections 3.1, 4.1; Appendix G.3).
- Healthcare groups are `tg13` intake/transfer, `tg14` payer authorization/appeals, `tg15` EHR quality/data governance, `tg16` clinical protocol decision support. Legal groups are `tg17` white-collar investigation review, `tg18` court disposition/financial entries, `tg19` regulatory licensing/compliance, `tg20` M&A review/negotiation. Finance uses `tg08–tg11`. Appendix B's Table 2 and Figure 5's caption disagree on some finance/ERP numbering; pin actual task IDs before splitting (GDPevo Appendix B, Table 2; Appendix E, Figure 5).
- Examples require evidence-grounded authorization/appeal handling (`tg14`), jurisdiction/date/case-status-sensitive court entries (`tg18`), and clinical decisions grounded in runtime protocols (`tg16`). Some clinical thresholds are already visible at test time. Reflection improves `tg14` from 51% to 71.33%, but few-shot learning lowers `tg18` from 48.36% to 39.83% and `tg16` from 64.25% to 58.92%. These use different model configurations, each averaged over three runs per task (GDPevo Appendix F, Table 5; Appendix F.2–F.4).
- Training separates few-shot access to gold answers, reflection with scores but no answers for three rounds, and self-evolution without answers or scores; fresh test agents consume the resulting persistent artifact. Existing cross-domain experiments cover CRM/ERP/finance, **not finance anchors repairing healthcare/legal evolution** (GDPevo Sections 4.1, 4.3).
- Every test task is run three times, with task-group macro-averaging. PLAN's avg@2, if applied to T2, departs from that native replication convention (GDPevo Section 4.1; Table 3).
- **Isolation and orchestration:** each evolution/test attempt has an independent workspace and raw trace. Stage-specific file/endpoint/feedback allowlists exclude answers outside supervised training, notes, evaluators, unrelated tasks, and prior attempts; the business environment runs separately through allowed endpoints. Each test process solves one task; each non-base test attempt uses the skill from its corresponding evolution attempt. The evaluation agent stages and pairs artifacts, invokes the deterministic grader, handles failures, and aggregates; it does not assign semantic scores. Hold its model/version, reasoning, prompt, permissions, retries, and aggregation fixed. Pin grader/rubric versions as a reproduction decision and preserve legitimate visible runtime protocols (GDPevo Appendix F.4; Appendices G.2–G.4).

### 9. Harness Updating Is Not Harness Benefit

This study separates how well a model updates harnesses from how much a task model benefits from updates. Updating varies less across tested evolvers than task-model benefit; benefits depend on baseline capability and task, with weaker execution and stronger-model ceilings both limiting gains. This supports testing capability, not assuming any named mid-tier model is optimal (Harness Updating Sections 3.3, 4.2–4.3).

**What the plan uses**

- Updating averages gains across three anchor agents; benefit takes the maximum across three anchor evolvers. Seven evolvers and six task models are studied. Haiku 4.5's gains are 2.4/3.6/15.1 percentage points on SWE-bench Verified/MCP-Atlas/SkillsBench; the largest SWE-bench Verified gain is Qwen3-235B's 19.3 percentage points. These are distinct from RHO's SWE-Pro results. Qwen3.5-9B is an evolver here, not evidence for PLAN's proposed Qwen3.5 task-model tier (Harness Updating Section 3.3; Table 1; Appendix B).
- Evaluation is an in-situ task stream: solve before using that task's evidence for subsequent updates. All three benchmarks permit skill edits; MCP-Atlas additionally permits `prompts/system.md` edits and append-only `memory/` JSONL updates; tools and evaluators remain fixed. This is narrower than arbitrary harness-code evolution and differs from evaluating a frozen final artifact on held-out tasks (Harness Updating Sections 4.1–4.2; Appendix B.3–B.4).
- The prior digest records `https://github.com/A-EVO-Lab/a-evolve/tree/release/harness-evolution` as PDF-annotation-derived. The supplied text only says source code is publicly available “at here”; neither that exact URL nor A-EVO-Lab ownership is verifiable in this text-only audit, which does not establish that the URL is false. The solve/evolve abstraction and prompts are plausible reusable components; this review does not establish that the code runs locally or supports the required isolation/edit scope (Harness Updating p.1; Appendix B; independent review finding 17).
- A concrete evidence-sensitive adherence rubric actually appears here: FOLLOWED, VIOLATED_COMMISSION, VIOLATED_OMISSION, REQUIRED_BUT_UNOBSERVED, NOT_APPLICABLE, INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE. It locks atomic instructions and requires trace evidence. HFR is the fraction of skill-loaded SkillsBench trajectories judged to follow the loaded skill, not an average of the six labels. Keep activation, applicability, omission after sufficient opportunity, early termination (REQUIRED_BUT_UNOBSERVED), and ambiguity (INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE) separate. Instruction-to-scalar aggregation and whether requirements remain fixed across evolution are study decisions, not fully supplied defaults (Harness Updating Appendix D.3; Tables 12–13).

### 10. Meta-Harness

Meta-Harness makes the full search archive—harness source, scores, and trajectories—available to a coding proposer. It can diagnose previous failures and branch from earlier candidates rather than only revise the current incumbent. This is reusable loop design, but oracle-bearing logs require redesign for the proposed restricted-feedback arms (Meta-Harness Section 3; Appendix D).

**What the plan uses**

- A Claude Code/Opus 4.6 proposer edits a Python harness interface; interface-valid candidates are evaluated and appended to the archive even when their scores regress; interface validation is not a validation dataset. Algorithm 1 returns a Pareto frontier after a fixed budget, rather than specifying PLAN's strict greedy improvement gate. Section 3 specifies final test-set evaluation of the Pareto frontier with test results hidden from the proposer; the TB experiment below is a same-set exception. Proposal counts vary by experiment: classification uses 20 iterations with two candidates; the TB worked example spans ten iterations (Meta-Harness Sections 3, 4.1; Appendix A).
- TB2 uses 89 tasks, with strong Terminus 2/KIRA seeds; reported evolved scores are 76.4% for Opus 4.6 and 37.6% for Haiku 4.5. This experiment intentionally uses the **same task set for discovery and final evaluation**, with manual/regex leakage checks, not a disjoint held-out protocol (Meta-Harness Section 4.3).
- The TB harness **inherits Terminus-KIRA's completion checklist**; the successful discovered change is environment bootstrapping. The worked search reports six prior regressions from completion-flow, prompt, or observation-processing edits, so those experiments do not establish a successful discovered completion checklist. These results do not prescribe a minimal ReAct seed, six-round pilot, or avg@2 promotion rule (Meta-Harness Appendix A, worked TB search; Appendix B.3).
- Search-archive retention is distinct from incumbent/deployed-state rollback: the proposer may inspect any prior parent, including candidates whose scores regress. The worked search references a separate earlier run. Declare whether such prior-run experience is permitted when claiming independent evolution seeds (Meta-Harness Section 3, Algorithm 1; Appendix A, iteration 10).

## Part D. Positioning this study

The defensible contribution is a controlled comparison of outcome judging, process judging, and retrospective self-preference under repeated harness evolution, measuring outcome-judge error, process–outcome alignment with separately validated process judgments, and held-out oracle performance, followed by a test of small in-domain and cross-domain acceptance anchors. Relative to SEAL's reported Atari experiments, this moves from policy/self-test co-evolution to language-agent harness search with fixed feedback mechanisms. SEAL's audit formulation already allows interactive rollouts and hidden code tests; PLAN Section 7 is best understood as its proposed harness adaptation, with no established missing-source dependency (SEAL pp.3–7; PLAN Section 7 and References). Relative to HASE, it freezes model weights and compares feedback regimes instead of jointly training the model and repairing an editable evaluator with explicit oracle counterexamples (HASE Sections 3–4). Relative to RHO, it tests a multi-round boundary beyond the reported one-round self-preference gains, retaining a faithful native-recipe reference before modifying budgets or model roles (RHO Sections 5, 7). The novelty should be this comparative mechanism-and-repair experiment, especially on rule-based healthcare/legal workflows, rather than the first no-verifier harness evaluation or first observation of proxy failure (Rethinking Section 4.2; GDPevo Sections 3–4).

The most consequential deltas are the missing SIGIL 5.2/5.7 passages, A3's mismatch with RHO's actual recipe, and the incompatible targets and scales of procedural compliance, relative preference, weighted GDPevo correctness, and binary outcome success under one unqualified gap metric. Correcting those definitions and matching compute/feedback are prerequisites for interpretation. Monotonic divergence and 50% cross-domain recovery remain prospective hypotheses, not established source results (SEAL p.3; SIGIL Section 4.1; RHO Appendices C–G; GDPevo Sections 4.1, 4.3; Rethinking Section 3).
